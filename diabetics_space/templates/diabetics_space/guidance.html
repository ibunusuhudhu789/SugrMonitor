Daily guidance

General habits that support steady blood sugar, and what to do if a reading is unusually high or low. This isn't a substitute for advice from your doctor or diabetes educator.

Everyday habits

Meals

Spacing carbohydrates evenly through the day, and pairing them with protein or fibre, tends to produce steadier readings than large single servings.

Movement

A short walk after eating — even ten minutes — measurably lowers the glucose spike that follows a meal.

Sleep

Poor or short sleep raises insulin resistance the next day. Consistent sleep and wake times help as much as total hours.

Stress

Stress hormones raise blood sugar independent of food. Brief breathing exercises or a change of scene can blunt this response.

Hydration

Dehydration concentrates glucose in the blood. Water is the safest choice — sugary drinks work against you here.

Testing routine

Testing at the same times each day — fasting, before and after meals — makes your trend line meaningful rather than noisy.

If a reading is low (below 70 mg/dL)

Common signs: shakiness, sweating, sudden hunger, dizziness, confusion, a fast heartbeat.

Follow the 15–15 rule: take 15 grams of fast-acting carbohydrate — glucose tablets, fruit juice, or regular soda — wait 15 minutes, then re-check. Repeat if you're still below 70. Once you're back in range, eat a small snack with protein if your next meal is more than an hour away. Treat a severe episode (confusion, inability to swallow, loss of consciousness) as an emergency.

If a reading is high

Common signs: increased thirst, frequent urination, fatigue, blurred vision, headache.

An occasional high reading after a large meal is usually not urgent — a walk and extra water can help, and your next fasting reading will show whether it settled. Readings that stay high for several checks in a row, or that come with nausea, vomiting, or breathing that feels laboured, need medical attention rather than home management — these can be signs of a more serious complication.

When to call your doctor

Bring your logged readings, HbA1c trend, and medication list to appointments — the dashboard and history pages here are built to be shown, not just kept. Contact your care team sooner if you notice a pattern of highs or lows at the same time of day, if a new medication seems to be affecting your numbers, or if you're unwell and unable to keep food or water down.
